<CaseText id="problem" className="pt-32" sectionHeading="Overview" title="6 days, 10 centres, a product you can log into.">
My team took a year of by-hand reporting and shipped a hosted, working product in a 6-day build sprint. You can open it in a browser and use it, instead of clicking through a Figma file.

I ran both tracks myself: the service design and the code. I co-designed with the people who live the problem, Link Workers at Seniors Active Living Centres (SALCs), and with staff at the Older Adult Centres' Association of Ontario (OACAO), the provincial body that funds those centres and collects their year-end data.

The part I'm proud of is the feat behind the screens: a messy service problem moving from discovery to co-design to a hosted build in one sprint, by pairing service design with AI-assisted building.
</CaseText>

<CaseText sectionHeading="The Problem" title="10 out of 10 centres compile the year-end report by hand.">
Every SALC I interviewed told me the same thing: reporting to OACAO once a year is a paper chase that eats days. Workers cross-reference paper binders, Excel sheets, and SurveyMonkey forms to reconstruct a year they already lived.

That time comes straight out of <Def definition="social-prescribing">Social Prescribing</Def>: the calls, the check-ins, the trust-building that are the actual job. Proving the work happened squeezes out the work itself.
</CaseText>

<CaseText className="pb-32" sectionHeading="The Challenge">
How do I give Link Workers more of the relational time that defines their role, **while making OACAO's funding case easier to prove at the same time?**
</CaseText>

<CaseGroup id="discovery" variant="discovery">
<CaseText sectionHeading="Discovery" title="Every centre tracks differently.">
I spoke with 10 SALCs over the winter. Each had its own way of managing clients, but OACAO's year-end report asked for data they didn't keep in one place, or didn't keep at all.

> "I don't know if you know all about the reporting process, but it's, it's like a three-day event to try and do the final report."

So the goal got specific: cut the cost of reporting without making everyday client work more bureaucratic.
</CaseText>

<CaseText sectionHeading="Constraint" title="The backstage was a privacy problem.">
I was handling personal health information, which in Ontario means two laws: PHIPA (the Personal Health Information Protection Act) and PIPEDA (Canada's federal privacy law). That set a hard line: useful reporting was fine, but OACAO could never become the owner of identifiable client records.

That constraint shaped the architecture later, and it shaped the service now. The centres keep their clients' identities; OACAO only ever sees numbers.
</CaseText>
</CaseGroup>

<CaseGroup id="decision" variant="discovery">
<CaseText sectionHeading="The Key Decision" title="Two roles, two permission scopes.">
Here is the service-level call everything else hangs on. LinkLog is really two products with one database between them. Link Workers hold identifiable client data locally and do the follow-up. OACAO sees only de-identified aggregates and does the funding math. Neither one sees the other's view.

The second decision fell out of the first. If the directory already knows a client's referrals, attendance, and outcomes, the year-end report has no business asking for any of it again. The report became a view of data the system already had, rather than a form to refill.

These are service decisions, the kind you make before you design a single screen. The execution came after.
</CaseText>

<CaseGroup id="implementation" variant="discovery">
<CaseText sectionHeading="The Build" title="A product you can log into, in 6 days.">
The stack is deliberately boring where it has to be trustworthy. Django and PostgreSQL on the back end, role-based access so the two scopes stay separated, hosted on AWS in **ca-central-1** so the data physically stays in Canada, with safe-harbour encryption on the fields that need it. That is what PHIPA and PIPEDA actually demand, and it is why I built a relational schema rather than a spreadsheet with extra steps.

AI is how a 4-person team shipped this in a week, so let me be precise about who did what. Codex wrote most of the backend, generated test suites, and pushed live images to AWS through Terraform. Claude Code reviewed the pull requests Codex opened and turned Figma frames into front-end code. Gemini Deep Research cross-referenced our implementation choices against the actual text of PHIPA and PIPEDA, so the privacy decisions were grounded in policy instead of guesswork.

None of this was hands-off. The importer is the clearest case: the fast path Codex generated assumed one rigid spreadsheet schema, co-design told me that was wrong, so I overrode it and rebuilt import to accept any structure. AI moved fast; the user-protecting calls stayed mine.

I shipped a running product over a blueprint on purpose, because a hosted demo answers questions a slide can't. You can log in as a Link Worker, import a real sheet, generate a real report, then switch to the OACAO view and watch the same data come back de-identified. The two permission scopes stop being a claim and become something you can test.
</CaseText>

<CaseText id="outcomes" sectionHeading="Outcomes" title="Where it actually stands.">
I'll be straight about the status, because the honest version is still a good one.

LinkLog runs as a hosted demo on AWS, and I presented it as a working product at the final review, not as mockups. OACAO took part in the co-design and came away interested in a pilot. No pilot has run yet; that is the next step, not a result I get to claim.

I also haven't measured impact, because there is nothing live to measure. What I designed toward is concrete: turning the year-end report from a multi-day reconstruction into a minutes-long review, and giving OACAO one consolidated, de-identified view in place of a SurveyMonkey scavenger hunt. The metrics that would prove it, time saved per worker and on-time CTT completion rate, are specified and waiting on a pilot.
</CaseText>

<CaseText id="closing" sectionHeading="Takeaways" title="What I'm taking from this.">
<CaseThought title="I can move fast with AI without outsourcing judgment.">
  The importer is the proof. Codex generated a clean, fast import flow that quietly assumed one spreadsheet schema, and a Link Worker in co-design asked whether her differently-shaped sheet would even load. The interviews had already told me it wouldn't, so I overrode the AI's default and rebuilt import to accept any structure.

  That is why I trust myself with these tools now. They write the code and move the week along; I keep the calls that protect the people using it. The judgment transfers to any team trying to ship real software fast without letting the model design the product.
</CaseThought>

<CaseThought title="Shipping a real product changed how the work landed.">
  I could have stopped at a polished prototype. Building a hosted product meant OACAO could log in and react to something real, and that is what turned a class project into a pilot conversation. The honest scorecard: a working product in 6 days, genuine OACAO interest, and no measured impact yet because the pilot hasn't run. I'd rather state that plainly than dress it up.
</CaseThought>
</CaseText>
